;; The formats on the Format Deck page (/formats). Edit this file and the page restyles itself.
;; Lines starting with ;; are notes and never show up on the page.
;;
;; Each format is a block that starts with ## and the format's name. The lines under it are:
;;   category: Contrast        the group it sits in. Spell it the same way in every format of that group.
;;   how: One or two sentences on how the format works.
;;   template: A line of the post. Put {a label} where the writer types something in.
;;                             One "template:" line per line of the post. An empty "template:" is a blank line.
;;   tip: One or two sentences of advice. (Optional.)
;;
;; The same {label} used twice is one blank that fills in twice (handy for callbacks).
;; The post must fit in 300 characters with about 40 characters in each blank. The test suite checks this.
;; In how and tip, a link looks like [text](/tracer) or [text](https://example.com).
;; Categories show up on the page in the order they first appear here, and the page keeps each category's formats together, so a format can go anywhere in this file.
;;
;; Rules for what goes in (same as the Mashup Machine's topics): no slurs, no named real people,
;; nothing about real tragedies, nothing explicit. Do not use em dashes or en dashes.

## Nobody / Me
category: Contrast
how: Describe a quiet, ordinary world, then show your own very specific, unrequested behavior.
template: Nobody:
template: Me: {an oddly specific thing you do}

## Expectation / Reality
category: Contrast
how: Pair a grand plan with what actually happened. The gap is the joke, so keep the plan sincere and the outcome small and exact.
template: Expectation: {the grand plan}
template: Reality: {the small, exact outcome}

## Then and Now
category: Contrast
how: Put a year from the past next to this year. The first line says how things were then. The second is a surprising way they have changed, or stayed exactly the same.
template: {a year in the past}: {what things were like then}
template: {this year}: {a surprising change, or a surprising lack of one}

## Translation Guide
category: Contrast
how: Take something polite people say and translate it into what they actually mean. It works best when the real meaning is petty and not mean.
template: "{a polite phrase}" means "{what it really means}"

## Two Types of People
category: Contrast
how: Split the world into two groups, where the second group is the point. Make the first side reasonable and the second delightfully specific.
template: There are two types of people in the world: {type one} and {type two}.

## We Are Not the Same
category: Contrast
how: Show what someone else does in a slightly sneaky or small way, then show what you do in the same setting, which is far bigger or more absurd. The second action is the joke.
template: You're in {his or her} DMs, I'm {something much bigger} {his or her} {a thing}. We are not the same.

## In This Economy
category: Contrast
how: Ask for two nice things at once, as if that were wildly unreasonable. The things should be small and ordinary, so the complaint about money feels out of proportion.
template: {a nice thing} AND {another nice thing}? In this economy?

## A Ten, But
category: Contrast
how: Give someone a perfect score, then name the one thing that spoils it. The dealbreaker works best when it is small, odd, and said completely straight.
template: {He's, she's, or they're} a 10, but {the specific dealbreaker}.

## (Seductively) X
category: Contrast
how: Put "(seductively)" in front of something that is not normally seductive, like a chore, a warning, or a piece of office news. The joke is the stage direction pretending the plain sentence is smoldering.
template: (seductively) {something not normally seductive}

## X (Flirting)
category: Contrast
how: Say something that is not normally flirty, then add "(flirting)" afterward, as if the label made it true. The flatter and more practical the line, the better the label works.
template: {something not normally flirty} (flirting)

## Sure Sex Is Great, But
category: Contrast
how: Compare sex to something else that feels good, and let the other thing win. It should be a pleasure almost everyone knows, like a nap or a perfect sandwich, that contrasts with sex in an interesting way. It can be better for just this one person. The joke is undercutting the idea that sex is the best feeling there is.
template: Sure sex is great, but {a different pleasure that wins}

## Award Ceremony
category: Lists
how: Give an award to an unlikely winner and explain it with a reason that is too exact. The reason is the joke.
template: And the award for {the award} goes to {the winner}, for {the reason}.

## Pros and Cons
category: Lists
how: A decision list where the con quietly cancels the pro, often by restating it. One line each, said calmly.
template: Thinking about {a big decision}.
template: Pros: {a lovely pro}
template: Cons: {a calm, devastating con}

## Fake Headline
category: Fake documents
how: Write a news headline where a small, ordinary event is treated as big news. Use headline grammar: present tense, short words, a semicolon or colon.
template: LOCAL {person} {does something ordinary}; {official reaction}

## One-Star Review
category: Fake documents
how: Review something that cannot be reviewed, or review a normal thing for the wrong reason. Stay sincere and a little aggrieved.
template: ★☆☆☆☆ {the thing being reviewed}
template: "{a review title}"
template: {the complaint}

## I Hope This Email Finds You
category: Fake documents
how: Take the stock opening of a work email and finish it with something that is not "well". The new ending can be a place, a mood, or a very specific situation.
template: I hope this email finds you {an unexpected state or place}.

## Overheard
category: Dialogue
how: Report one line of dialogue with no context. The missing context does the work.
template: Overheard at {a place}:
template: "{a line out of context}"

## Interview Detour
category: Dialogue
how: In a job interview, ask a long, rambling question about a very weird or very specific subject. The interviewer gently steers you back.
template: Me: {a long, rambling question about a very weird or specific subject}
template: Interviewer: I meant questions about {the interview topic}.

## Professor X Asks
category: Dialogue
how: Professor X asks what your superpower is. You name a power, and his reply twists his own name, puns on the power, or does something else you did not see coming.
template: Professor X: What's your superpower?
template: Me: {a superpower}
template: Professor X: {a surprising reply, such as a pun on the power}

## Brain Dialogue
category: Dialogue
how: You argue with your own brain. The brain brings up something old or odd at the worst time, and your reply never stops it.
template: Brain: {an unhelpful thought}
template: Me: {a reasonable reply}
template: Brain: {the escalation}

## Talking Object
category: Dialogue
how: Say something to an object and let it answer back. Give the object a human flaw such as pride, laziness, or passive aggression.
template: Me: {what I say to the object}
template: {the object}: {what it says back}

## The Goth Version
category: Dialogue
how: Ask how much for the goth version of something. The reply names a real thing that turns out to be exactly that, spiky or dark or dramatic. Whoever answers should be someone who would know, like a pet store clerk. For example: "me: how much for the goth guinea pig / pet store clerk: that's a hedgehog"
template: me: how much for the goth {a thing for sale}
template: {someone who would know}: that's a {the real thing that fits}

## The Saying, Answered
category: Dialogue
how: Quote a familiar saying, then follow it with a reply that takes it literally, disagrees, or ruins it. Keep the saying sincere and the reply flat.
template: "{a well-known saying}" {a surprising response}

## Pun With a Question
category: Wordplay
how: The classic question-and-answer pun. A short question and an instant answer.
template: {a question}
template: {the pun answer}

## Dictionary Entry
category: Wordplay
how: A new word, a fake definition, and a sentence that uses it. It works best when the word sounds like it already exists.
template: {the word} (n.): {the definition}
template: "{a sentence using it}"

## Portmanteau
category: Wordplay
how: Blend two words into a new one and define it. If people say "oh no" at the blend, you are on the right track.
template: {word one} + {word two} = {the blend}
template: (n.) {what it means}

## Rename It
category: Wordplay
how: Give something ordinary a new, more honest name. The reason is optional, but it is often where the joke lives.
template: Renaming {the thing} to "{the new name}" because {the reason}.

## Is Just
category: Wordplay
how: Define one thing as another thing that is surprising but, once you think about it, fits. The fit is the joke, so keep it short.
template: {the first thing} is just {a surprising but fitting second thing}.

## Classic Blunder
category: Wordplay
how: Borrow the famous speech from The Princess Bride about the classic blunders and name a pitfall that is either surprising or silly, ideally with a grain of truth. The more seriously you say it, the better.
template: You just committed one of the classic blunders: {a surprising or silly pitfall}

## You're Telling Me
category: Wordplay
how: Act shocked that a creature or person did something to a thing, where their name or nature is the pun. The canonical version is "You're telling me a shrimp fried this rice?"
template: You're telling me {a creature or person} {a past tense verb} this {a thing}?

## Only If It's From
category: Wordplay
how: Borrow the champagne rule. Insist that something only counts if it comes from one exact place, and that everything else is a lesser version. It works best when the place name is surprising, or sounds like part of the thing. The canonical version is "It's only champagne if it's from the Champagne region of France, otherwise it's just sparkling wine."
template: It's only {a fancy thing} if it's from the {a region} region of {a larger place}, otherwise it's just sparkling {a plain version}.

## What Idiot Called It
category: Wordplay
how: Complain that a thing was given a bad name when a much better one was right there. The better name should usually be something that already exists and would still fit the thing literally, which is what makes it so strange that nobody used it.
template: What idiot called it {the bad name} when {the obvious better name} was right there?

## Couldn't Think of the Word
category: Wordplay
how: Admit you could not think of the word for something, so you used a different one. The substitute should be wrong, but more descriptive than the real word. For example: "I couldn't think of the word for tortilla so I just called it a burrito skin."
template: I couldn't think of the word for {the thing} so I just called it {the substitute}.

## Charlie Brown
category: Wordplay
how: End a phrase or a title with ", Charlie Brown," so it sounds like a line from a Peanuts special. The joke is the phrase suddenly becoming something Lucy or Linus would say to him.
template: {a phrase or title}, Charlie Brown

## Rule of Three
category: Escalation
how: Two items set a pattern and the third breaks it. The pattern is the setup and the break is the punchline.
template: {first item}, {second item}, and {third item}.

## Anti-Climax
category: Escalation
how: Build up something grand, then land on something tiny. The bigger the build, the smaller the letdown can be.
template: {a grand build-up}, only to {a tiny letdown}.

## Running Bit
category: Escalation
how: Say the same calm sentence in a worsening situation. The phrase stays fixed while the world falls apart around it. You type the phrase once and it fills in twice.
template: Day 1: {a small problem}. I said "{the repeated phrase}."
template: Day 9: {a much worse problem}. I said "{the repeated phrase}."

## Hill to Die On
category: Escalation
how: List the things you will defend to the end: two serious principles, then one tiny petty one.
template: Hills I will die on:
template: - {a serious principle}
template: - {another serious principle}
template: - {a tiny petty thing}

## How To
category: Escalation
how: Give instructions that start normal and end up somewhere else. Each step should be a little odder than the last.
template: How to {reach a goal}:
template: 1. {a normal step}
template: 2. {a slightly odd step}
template: 3. {a step that makes no sense}

## On My Until I
category: Escalation
how: Say that someone keeps doing something to one of your things until you finally do something about it. It is spicy and hard to get right. The best versions use blanks that belong to the same world, so the action, the thing and the result all feel related.
template: {He or she} {a verb}s on my {a thing} until I {a related result}.

## Secret Third Thing
category: Escalation
how: Name two options that people treat as the only choices, then reject both. The punchline is always the same: "but a secret third thing." You only fill in the two rejected choices, and the joke is how well they work as a pair.
template: Not {the first choice}, not {the second choice}, but a secret third thing.

## Nature Documentary
category: Tiny scenes
how: Narrate a mundane human like a wildlife film. The calmer the narrator, the funnier the tiny stakes.
template: Here we see the {a creature} in its natural habitat, {doing something mundane}.
template: {a dramatic narration line}

## Dear Diary
category: Tiny scenes
how: A diary entry about a long, strange situation with a very small observation at the end.
template: Dear diary, day {a number} of {a situation}.
template: {the observation}

## Future Archaeologists
category: Tiny scenes
how: Imagine people in the far future misreading an ordinary object. The wrong conclusion has to be reasonable, which is what makes it funny.
template: Future archaeologists will find {an ordinary object} and conclude {the wrong conclusion}.

## Confession
category: Tiny scenes
how: Admit something small and specific with the weight of a major confession.
template: I have a confession:
template: {the confession}

## LaCroix
category: Tiny scenes
how: Describe the sparkling water in a silly way, usually by how faint its flavor is. Say it as if you were explaining a real discovery.
template: LaCroix is what happens when {a silly way of describing the drink}.

## Your Honor
category: Tiny scenes
how: Write a setup, such as a phrase, a complaint, or a confession, and end it with "your honor." The sudden move to a courtroom is the punchline, because it makes the setup sound like testimony.
template: {a phrase, complaint, or other setup}, your honor.
